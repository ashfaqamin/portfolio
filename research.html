Master's thesis

The effectiveness of mobile virtual reality as a means for pain distraction

Research shows VR is a useful medium for pain distraction, especially in acute pain. Our earlier studies in the Pain Studies Lab, SIAT, SFU found VR's effectiveness in chronic-pain management using traditional high-tech VR systems like Oculus Rift. My research tests the effectiveness of the low-cost and more widely accessible mobile VR systems like Cardboard VR.

Two studies on Cardboard VR

Study 01

The effectiveness of Cardboard VR with chronic-pain patients

Cardboard VR gave patients real pain distraction — though the Rift's effect on remembered pain was stronger.

This repeated-measures study in a clinical setting examined how effective Cardboard VR is with actual pain patients playing a VR game, compared to an Oculus Rift. 30 participating patients volunteered to play InMind on the Rift and the Cardboard. I measured Present Pain Intensity (PPI) and Retroactive Pain Intensity (RPI — pain in the last 10 minutes). For RPI the effect of the Rift was profound, and the difference between the two HMDs was significant. Immersion and single-measure pain distraction were also significant. However, Cardboard was still capable of providing significant pain distraction compared to the pre-VR (no VR) condition.

Study 02

Immersion in mobile VR compared to a traditional HMD

Cardboard VR reached an acceptable level of immersion, despite its small screen and simplicity.

The study aimed to find the difference in levels of immersion between a Cardboard VR and a traditional head-mounted display — the Oculus Rift DK2. Three groups of participants played Cryoblast, a VR pain-management game, on Cardboard, Oculus Rift and Desktop (control). Jennett et al.'s Immersive Experience Questionnaire was used to measure immersion. The results showed that Cardboard VR, despite its simplicity and small screen size, is capable of providing an acceptable level of immersion compared to the Oculus Rift's larger screen. However, examining the underlying factors of immersion (control, real-world dissociation, and so on) showed differences between the two viewers.
