Academic research / Cardboard VR with chronic-pain patients

Simon Fraser University · 2016

Effectiveness of Cardboard VR with chronic-pain patients

At the Pain Studies Lab of Simon Fraser University, we mostly work on VR projects for pain patients. My research interest is affordable mobile VR and its use in pain distraction for chronic-pain patients. This study was conducted in September 2016.

Experimental design

This study used a repeated-measures design in which each patient played InMind on both Cardboard and Oculus Rift in a random order. A random uniform distribution ensured half of the participants used Cardboard first and the other half used Oculus first. The primary independent variable was the HMD type.

The primary dependent variable was retroactive pain intensity (PI). There are two kinds of PI — Present Pain Intensity and Retroactive Pain Intensity (pain felt in the last 10 minutes). I used the retroactive PI to measure patients' perceived pain intensity while inside the virtual environment. PI was measured three times: before playing the VR game; after playing on one HMD; and after playing again on the other HMD. Patients reported PI by filling out visual analog scale (VAS) questionnaires — one variation for the Pre-VR condition and another for the Post-VR condition. Immersion was a secondary dependent variable, captured through several questions on the VAS questionnaire.

Results

Retroactive pain intensity

Retroactive pain intensity measured the pain felt by participants during the last 10 minutes inside the VR world. A repeated-measures ANOVA revealed the retroactive pain intensity across the three conditions was statistically significant, F(2, 56) = 11.007, p<0.05. Mauchly's test indicated the assumption of sphericity was not violated, χ2(2) = 3.842, p = 0.146. Effect size was medium, ωHMD = 0.5.

Bonferroni post hoc tests revealed a significant difference between the Pre-VR and Oculus Rift conditions, CI.95 = −17.773 (lower), −4.027 (upper), p = 0.001. There was also a significant difference between the Pre-VR and Cardboard VR conditions, CI.95 = −11.516 (lower), −0.151 (upper), p = 0.043. The difference in pain intensity between Oculus Rift and Cardboard VR was also significant, though only marginally, CI.95 = −10.131 (lower), −4.027 (upper), p = 0.050. There was no significant interaction effect for the order of use of the HMDs, F(2, 56) = 0.348, p = 0.708.

Present pain intensity

A repeated-measures ANOVA revealed that exposure to VR had a significant effect on present pain intensity too, F(2, 58) = 5.090, p<0.05. The assumption of sphericity was satisfied, χ2(2) = 0.606, p = 0.739. The effect size here was small, ωHMD = 0.3. This differs slightly from previous studies: an earlier within-subjects study of 20 participants comparing present and retroactive pain intensity between a VR (Oculus DK1) and an MBSR (Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction) condition found the difference in present pain intensity was not significant. In this study, however, participants used two VR HMDs back-to-back. The pairwise comparison using Bonferroni post hoc analysis showed the difference between Pre-VR and Oculus Rift was significant, CI.95 = −11.11 (lower), −0.889 (upper), p = 0.017. The difference between Pre-VR and Cardboard was not significant, CI.95 = −8.263 (lower), 1.263 (upper), p = 0.216.

Immersion

The difference in immersion levels between the two VR conditions was significant, F(1, 29) = 7.757, p = 0.009; effect size ωHMD = 0.42.
